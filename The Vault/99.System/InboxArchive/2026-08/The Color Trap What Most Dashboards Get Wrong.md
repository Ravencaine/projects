Surely, dashboards might look lively with all those colors — but is it really delivering the message you want to deliver? Color is not just for decorating visuals. In fact, using color without intention can often be **misleading and distracting**.

Therefore, it is appropriate to consider once more:

### How will color influence my analysis?

For example, you wish to visualize **total sales by month**.

![Comparison of two monthly sales charts — the left chart uses uniform colors for all months, while the right chart highlights February and May in red to draw focus.](99.System/Attachments/Comparison_of_two_monthly_sales_charts_—_the_left_chart_uses_uniform_colors_for_all_months,_while_th.webp)

Comparison of two monthly sales charts — the left chart uses uniform colors for all months, while the right chart highlights February and May in red to draw focus.

If you look at the left bar chart or line chart, where does your eyes go first? January? February? November? December? Or may be none of them.

Now look at the **right side**. Can you tell the difference between them?

I am guiding you to look at February and May. Every time you look at the second chart, you’ll notice those two months. That’s not magic — that’s **intentional color design**.

Before I go off, lets see few points to focus on while its about the use of color in your dashboard. Let’s not make the same misatke again.

1. **Be Selective:** If you use too many colors, nothing stands out. If everything shouts, the audience does not know where to look. Use color sparingly and only when needed.
2. **Maintain Consistency:** Once a specific meaning is assigned to a color, it should be preserved consistently. Switching colors for fun can make your message confusing — your audience will assume the color change means something significant.
3. **Think Inclusivity:** Did you know that around 8% of men and 0.5% of women experience color blindness? The majority of those affected have difficulty distinguishing between red and green, so relying on these colors together — especially to indicate “good” and “bad” — can unintentionally exclude part of your audience. If your branding requires the use of red and green, add extra cues such as icons or clear labels to communicate meaning. Alternatively, consider using color pairs like blue and orange, which are much more accessible and provide a friendlier, more inclusive experience for everyone.
4. **Consider the mood:** Colors convey emotion. Are you aiming for a bold, clinical vibe (hello, black!) or something softer and more thoughtful? Choose a palette that supports your message.
5. **Brand wisely:** If you are using brand colors, pick one or two as your “look here!” shades and keep the rest subtle. If your brand palette does not pop, don’t be afraid to reach for a contrasting color or a classic bold black.

Colorful and charming (OMG!) dashboard is good if it tells you what you are looking at. Your manager or CEO does not have ample time to understand and reads your data. Use the **3–30–300 rule**.
