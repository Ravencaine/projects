But Excel already has a built-in forecasting tool—and most people never realize it's there. Here's how to use it. The Forecast Sheet tool is currently only available in Excel for Windows. Excel for Mac and Excel for the web don't include this feature, but they do support certain forecasting functions, so you can still build forecasts manually and create charts using standard Excel tools. Before you get started: Set up your data Let Excel spot patterns in your timeline When you track data over time, patterns often repeat themselves. Ice cream sales naturally rise during hot summer months and dip during the winter, garden harvests follow similar seasonal cycles, and retail store revenue predictably spikes during the holiday shopping rush. This repeating pattern is called "seasonality," and Excel's Forecast Sheet tool is designed to detect it automatically without you needing to build complex formulas. Before launching the tool, make sure your dataset follows a few simple layout rules: Two parallel columns: Your dataset needs two columns—one for dates or time intervals, one for values. Consistent timeline intervals: Use regular spacing like daily, monthly, or yearly entries. Sorted data: Make sure everything is in chronological order. It also helps to format your data as an Excel table ( Ctrl+T or Insert > Table ). This lets Excel automatically expand your range if you add new rows later. Also, Excel can handle some missing data, but clean and consistent timelines produce better forecasts. Finally, the Forecast Sheet tool works best when you have at least two full cycles of history (for example, 24 months of monthly data) as a general rule of thumb. But if you have less, you can manually override the algorithm's automatic detection—I'll explain how in the next section. To fix this and perform the manual override I mentioned earlier, open the Options section by clicking the arrow at the bottom left of the dialog box. This unlocks the hidden settings that customize how the algorithm handles your specific timeline: Manually set seasonality: Locate the Seasonality section, switch from "Detect Automatically" to Set Manually , then enter a value that matches your cycle length (such as 12 for annual). This forces Excel to map your data accurately, helping the tool detect and display seasonal patterns instead of a flat trend line. Adjust the confidence intervals: The options panel also lets you toggle Confidence Intervals , which adds upper and lower confidence lines showing the likely range of future values. Uncheck this box if you just want a simple forecast view. Determine how gaps are filled in: You can also choose how Excel handles missing data—either by estimating values ( interpolation ) or treating them as zeros . Close Once you've tweaked these options to match your dataset, there are two more things to check: Decide on your chart type: Choose your visual preference by clicking either the line chart or column chart icon in the top-right corner of the window. Line charts work best for showing fluid, ongoing seasonal trends, while column charts are great for discrete monthly blocks. Choose the forecast length: Adjust the Forecast End date. Excel defaults to projecting only a few intervals, but you can change the date to look six months, a year, or even longer into the future, depending on how far ahead you want to plan. If you need deeper analytical context for a presentation, check Include forecast statistics . This prompts Excel to generate a separate table mapped out with advanced smoothing coefficients and mathematical error metrics.
