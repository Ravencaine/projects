> A system that covers the full research pipeline, without paywalls

I built a personal AI-powered research stack with nothing but free tools Close Close By Nolen Jonker Published Mar 6, 2026, 7:31 AM EST Nolen began their writing career in 2019, with three years dedicated to editing the Creative section at MakeUseOf . Their expertise lies at the crossroads of technology and creativity, covering areas like photography, video editing, and graphic design. Outside of work, you'll often find Nolen diving into a good book, writing their own stories, or playing video games. Sign in to your XDA account I realized the most frustrating part about my research system is that I use a lot of freemium and paywalled tools. I don’t mind freemium tools; a good chunk of my productivity and graphics stack comprises apps with paywalled features. But when it comes to research, there’s less room for experimentation or flexibility. Every time I hit a paywall, I have to either find a workaround, sign up for yet another trial, or abandon the source altogether, and that just doesn’t work as well for research where timeliness and accuracy are important. I’m of the stance that information should not only be easy to access, but also easy to make sense of . The raw content is out there, but when the tools that help you search, summarize, and organize are paywalled, it creates an unnecessary barrier to actually understanding what you find. This is why I put together a neat little research stack of the best free tools that let me find sources easily, and actually comprehend and organize the information I’m working with. And they don’t hide their most important features behind paywalls… Brave leads the way Scouting with Leo Close My browser of choice is Brave, and I practically live it. I love that it blocks ads and trackers, but Brave’s real secret weapon is its AI assistant, Leo . This is such a handy yet overlooked feature that pretty much carries most of my researching experience. And, of course in Brave fashion, it gives you the option to enable/disable it and configure its relevance in your browsing experience. It automatically picks the best model for the job based on your query, including top models such as Claude 4.5 Haiku and GPT-OSS 20B, but you can also select your own default model. Leo uses the Brave Search API to access real-time and up-to-date information online. When enabled, it automatically generates an AI summary at the top of the search layout, from where you can start a thread and dive into deeper research. This is where most of my research in the browser happens, and Leo sites each of its sources with a direct weblink. It’s important to verify the sources themselves because Leo only reports what it scans, regardless of whether the content itself is accurate. My favorite part about Leo is its natural language processing and contextual understanding. My prompt can be a hot mess, only vaguely describing what I’m looking for, complete with spelling and grammar errors, and Leo knows exactly where to lead me. It also has a separate chat interface, similar to Google’s Gemini, which is more conversational, lets you upload files, and lets you edit its responses. VPN, Built-in AL Assistant See at Brave Expand Collapse Khoj helps me make sense of my sources This is the next step in my research process Khoj is a more recent find for me and quickly solidified itself as a viable open-source NotebookLM alternative. It uses the same RAG-based technology that analyzes the sources you provide, including weblinks and uploaded files, and then retrieves and summarizes the content according to your prompt instructions. After gathering sources and information from Leo, I funnel them into my Khoj knowledge base and start interacting with them. I like using a retrieval-augmented generation AI because I can combine multiple sources in one chat, talk through what I just found, start forming research questions, pull in other notes and documents from my local stack, and gradually build context around the topic. Leo does have RAG, but Khoj’s RAG capabilities are just superior to Leo’s for deep specialized knowledge management because it’s built for in-depth indexing and semantic search over large data sets. Plus, it still has web access so I can keep pulling in external sources if needed. Khoj also has Agents and Automation, so I can create custom personas for topics that require it (for example, a design instructor), and I can automate recurring queries. Khoj AI See at Khoj Expand Collapse A local LLM for the final synthesis LM Studio is my last stop I’ve been using local models through LM Studio for a while now, and this is the last stop in my research process where my research turns into usable knowledge. Also, it’s local, so I have ownership of all the conversations and note-taking that happens in my local LLM. This makes it more suitable to sensitive topics like health and finances where I might need to provide some personal information for further understanding. The knowledge stacks I’ve created in Khoj goes directly into my local model, and here, I don’t have to worry about my personal data or censorship anymore. And, most importantly, zero paywalls - I get to use the models to their full capacity. LM Studio also gives me a bunch of controls to fine-tune the behavior of my AI. I convert most of the conversations to Markdown files using the LM Studio Conversation Converter , so I can easily store and iterate on them in a notes app like Obsidian.
